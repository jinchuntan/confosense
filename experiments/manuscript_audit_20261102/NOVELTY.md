# What ConfoSense adds to the literature

**A monitor can score more detections precisely when it gives operators fewer warnings during sensor freezes.** To test this, we held the models, calibration, alarm rule, test timestamps, injected readings and event-credit window fixed. We changed the history supplied to the one-step forecast. In one replay the forecast used the original, undisturbed meter history. In the operational replay the forecast saw each faulty reading as it arrived. Both replays scored the same observed readings. This is a retrospective controlled experiment on injected faults.

For the submitted interval model, the event matcher credited 160 of 497 freezes with original history and 166 with operational history. First alarms during the changed readings fell from 76 to 14. Of the 76 formerly timely alarms, 38 moved later, 25 disappeared from the entire credit window and 13 stayed timely. One event that had been late became timely. The separate XGBoost comparison shows the same direction in both verified periods. Across the two periods, event credit went from 215 to 242 out of 912 freezes, while timely alerts went from 109 to 31. The two models share some events. These descriptive comparisons do not establish performance on natural failures. [Paired counts and verified scope](README.md#paired-replay-on-the-manuscript-models)

## One building, one freeze, two interpretations

On the recorded BDG2 clock, the replay held the Hog_assembly_Dona electricity reading at 439.757 kWh from 23:00 on 25 October 2016 through 02:00 the next day. At 01:00 the same reading exceeded the upper prediction bound with original history (388.848 kWh) and fell inside the interval with faulty history (285.164–525.429 kWh). At 03:00 the meter resumed normal readings at 198.754 kWh. It now fell below the operational forecast's lower bound (208.074 kWh), triggering the first operational alarm. Both arms scored a hit because the scoring window remained open.

## Which parts have precedents?

We searched for building consumption alarms, frozen sensors, anomalous autoregressive inputs, timing-tolerant event scoring, maintenance records and alarm earliness, then inspected the sources closest to those questions. This is a targeted comparison. The search does not establish worldwide priority. The journal-method references below all appeared in 2024 or later in non-MDPI journals. We used JCR journal impact factor quartiles, with an identified ranking year. The 2026 paper uses the available 2025 ranking. Quartiles belong to journals and categories. For example, Energy and Buildings was Q1 in Construction & Building Technology and Q2 in Energy & Fuels in 2024. Data descriptions are identified separately.

| Study | Evidence the authors report | Distinction from this experiment |
|---|---|---|
| Stjelja et al. (2024), *Energy and Buildings*, Q1, 2024 | Applies EnbPI and XGBoostLSS to heating and electricity. The public notebook includes lagged outdoor temperature and evaluates whether a day receives an alarm. The paper tests artificial consumption anomalies and robustness to noise in training. | Establishes building consumption monitoring with conformal intervals. We found no matched comparison of the same frozen meter events with and without faulty target history and first alarms classified relative to recovery. [Journal article](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enbuild.2024.114249) · [Public implementation](https://github.com/sirdawar/ProbabilisticBuildingAnomaly/tree/7f2b651dd7124233e34ac40bb39c1831da31c1d9) · [Journal ranking](https://www.iit.comillas.edu/publicacion/info_revista/en/73/Energy_and_Buildings) |
| Shen et al. (2024), *Knowledge-Based Systems*, Q1, 2024 | AFMF explicitly treats anomalies entering the forecast input and uses progressive adjacent masking. Its released evaluator can compare masked predictions and optionally apply point adjustment when scoring. | The paper establishes that anomalous history can undermine detection. Its evidence concerns a forecasting intervention across benchmarks. We trace the same individual freezes to timely alarms, late alarms or misses with a fixed detector and unchanged scoring. | Salles et al. (2024), *Computers & Industrial Engineering*, Q1, 2024 | SoftED gives detections partial credit according to their temporal proximity to reference events. | Establishes that temporal tolerance matters to evaluation. We hold the credit rule constant. Changing what the predictor can observe sends broad event credit and during-fault warnings in opposite directions. [Journal article](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cie.2024.110728) · [Journal ranking](https://www.iit.comillas.edu/publicacion/info_revista/en/806/computers-industrial-engineering) |
| Roelofs et al. (2026), *Energy*, Q1, 2025 | Evaluates early warnings on district heating substations using service reports, eventwise scores and lead time against incident reports. Section 3.2 describes possible onsets assessed from the measurements and anomaly ends assigned four hours after maintenance. | Establishes a stronger type of operational validation than synthetic faults alone. It asks how far ahead of a report the detector warns. We isolate whether changing the forecast history moves the first warning past the end of a known freeze. The authors' assigned endpoints cannot by themselves timestamp sensor restoration. Within these four studies, we found no report of the paired reversal quantified above. The research contribution is an empirical finding about how to evaluate warnings, with a reproducible event-by-event audit. A method paper claiming invention of conformal alarms, repeated-value rules, masking of suspect inputs, or time-aware metrics would overstate this evidence.

## What the HouseZero incident can establish

For the 12 September 2024 Server 2 patch, the authors report 72 filtered minutes and restoration at **13:47 Boston time**. We checked all 1,440 released timestamps that day across the same 20 diagnostic channels the processing code uses (17 zone temperatures, outdoor temperature, wind speed and wind direction). All 20 readings are absent in two runs, **10:51–11:35** and **13:21–13:46** inclusive. This totals 71 minutes. Nineteen channels have readings at 13:47. The 72-minute publication count describes the subsystem filter and the 71-minute count describes simultaneous missingness in the final release. Their difference does not identify a fault-start time. The maintenance entry concerns a server and several channels can freeze together. [Data article, Table 2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-026-07720-3) · [Published year](https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.30260233.v1) · [Gap check](results/housezero_20240912.csv)

The authors' public processing repository contains code and a license. The code saves original acquisition timestamps, values, Quality and QualityGood in an intermediate Step1_(raw) export. Later steps drop bad-quality readings, resample time, flag synchronized repeats and remove flagged values. The public annual deposit supplies the processed streams. Inferring a held value from the preceding observation would manufacture the signal we are trying to test. [Processing code snapshot](https://github.com/Harvard-CGBC-at-GSD/HouseZero-one-year-dataset-June2024---May2025-Processing-Code/tree/538759276fd57ed643dfd78330490928cd5381f7)

**Decision.** Proceed with the paper's controlled-benchmark claim now. A natural incident is optional for that scope and necessary for a claim about what the live detector did on a documented incident. To run the latter test, an affected channel's pre-filter observations and acquisition quality flags around the interruption, independent timing information, and comparable healthy data would allow the alert to be evaluated against restoration. Even a successful single case would support an example rather than a field-wide performance rate. A paired clean-history comparison on that natural failure would additionally need a credible independent reference for the unobserved readings.
